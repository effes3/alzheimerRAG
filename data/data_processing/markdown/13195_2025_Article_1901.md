## Genetic insights into drug targets for alzheimer's disease: integrative multiomics analysis

Xiaoduo Liu 1*† , Jia Chen 2,3† , Shaojiong Zhou 4† and Jianfeng Lin 5*

## Abstract

Background The development of effective disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer's disease (AD) remains a critical unmet need. While Mendelian randomization (MR) has been leveraged to identify genetic variants to accelerate AD target discovery, previous studies have been limited by narrow phenotypic coverage, insufficient multiomics validation, and inadequate mechanistic exploration. This study aims to overcome these limitations via comprehensive MR to identify robust therapeutic targets.

Methods We performed an integrative multiomics MR analysis leveraging over 50 genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets spanning AD, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers (Aβ42, p-tau), neuroimaging endophenotypes, cognitive traits, and risk factors. Blood/CSF/brain protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) from large-scale proteomics studies were analyzed to identify druggable targets. A rigorous validation cascade was subsequently performed: Bayesian colocalization was performed to assess whether the same variants are associated with the protein and other traits; summary-data-based MR was performed to distinguish pleiotropy from linkage; mediation analysis was performed to quantify biomarker-driven causal pathways; integrated analysis of multiomics (single-cell RNA-seq and proteome) data was performed to resolve cellular specificity, and (PPI) interaction networks were generated; phenome-wide MR (Phe-MR) was performed across 679 traits to evaluate on-target side effects; and structure-based druggability screening was conducted.

Results Proteome-wide MR analysis revealed 15 potential drug targets for AD; six of these targets (PILRA, GRN, ACE, TIMD3, TREM2) were validated as Tier 1 (highest-confidence targets with external validation and causal consistency). Mediation analysis revealed that IDUA reduced the risk of AD through Aβ42 and p-tau in the CSF, whereas Siglec-7/9 increased the risk of AD through p-tau in the CSF. ## Introduction

The  increasing  global  burden  of  Alzheimer's  disease (AD)-a  progressive  neurodegenerative  disorder  characterized by memory loss, cognitive decline, and pathological amyloid plaques/neurofibrillary tangles with complex  multifactorial  causes-poses  a  growing  public health  risk  [1].  However,  no  disease-modifying  therapies  have  been  shown  to  halt  AD-associated  cognitive decline.  The  number  of  people  in  the  U.S.  aged  65  and older  with  AD  is  expected  to  skyrocket  in  the  future; by  2060,  this  number  could  reach  13.8  million  [1].  The recent conditional approval of anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies (e.g., lecanemab, aducanumab), which target amyloid  plaques  to  promote  their  clearance,  validates  the principle  of  target-driven  therapeutic  development,  and this approach provides a modest clinical benefit for some patients  [2,  3].  However,  the  widespread  application  of these drugs is significantly hampered by their prohibitive costs, complex administration regimens, safety concerns (such as amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs)), and, crucially, their marginal efficacy in terms of slowing disease  progression  [2-4].  These  limitations  collectively underscore the  urgent  and  unmet  need  for  mechanistically diverse, accessible, and efficacious therapeutic alternatives targeting pathways for biomarkers of AD.

Human proteins serve as fundamental biological effectors  and  represent  prime  druggable  targets  for  therapeutic  intervention,  as  they  directly  mediate  cellular

processes and disease pathogenesis [5]. The human proteome  exhibits  significant  compartmentalization;  proteins  within  the  brain  parenchyma  and  cerebrospinal fluid  (CSF)  are  inherently  more  closely  related  to  the underlying  pathology  of  central  nervous  system  (CNS) neurodegenerative  diseases  such  as  AD,  thus  reflecting the local biochemical environment and disease processes. Conversely,  the  peripheral  blood  proteome  may  offer a less direct view of CNS-specific mechanisms, but it is considerably  more  accessible  for  large-scale  screening, diagnosis,  and  therapeutic  monitoring,  and  it  remains invaluable for translational applications. Recent efforts to map protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) across blood, CSF,  and  brain  tissues  have  provided  unprecedented genetic leverage for causal inference via Mendelian randomization  (MR),  which  is  a  method  that  harnesses germline genetic variations to minimize confounding and reverse  causation  [6,  7].  Although  proteome-wide  MR has revolutionized target discovery in neurodegenerative diseases,  MR  studies  on  AD  have  been  superficial  thus far [8-10]. Prior investigations have narrowly focused on core AD clinical diagnosis as the primary endpoint, thus overlooking  the  critical  opportunity  to  explore  causal links to the rich landscape of preclinical AD biomarkers (e.g.,  Aβ  and  p-tau),  detailed  structural  and  functional neuroimaging  endophenotypes  (e.g.,  hippocampal  atrophy,  white  matter  hyperintensities,  network  connectivity),  and  the  trajectories  of  specific  cognitive  domain impairments that define the clinical syndrome [10, 11].

To bridge this gap, the aim of this study was to conduct multiomics MR analyses of AD by integrating data from over 50 genome-wide association studies (GWASs) spanning  AD  diagnosis,  CSF  biomarkers  (Aβ/p-tau),  structural  neuroimaging,  and  cognitive  domains.  In  addition to  identifying  therapeutic  targets,  we  pioneered  causal mediation models that elucidated biomarker-driven pathways,  revealed  target  cellular  hubs  at  single-cell resolution  and  performed  systematic  safety  profiling. This comprehensive framework was designed to identify highly prioritized therapeutic targets with robust genetic evidence for causality, to provide a clear biological foundation in the context of the AD pathological cascade and to significantly reduce the risk of failure in costly clinical development owing to efficacy or safety issues.

## Methods

The overall study design, which integrates pQTL and outcome  GWAS  data  with  sensitivity  analyses,  validation, mediation, and mechanistic exploration, is presented in Fig. 1. Details of the methodology and study participants are  provided  below.  This  study  was  reported  in  accordance with the STROBE-MR guidelines (Table S1) [12].

## Study population and datasets Proteomic data sources

We  used  summary-level  plasma  pQTL  data  from  the deCODE consortium of Ferkingstad et al. (Table S2-S3) [5].  Additionally,  pQTL  data  for  CSF  and  brain  tissue were  obtained  from  Yang  et  al.  (Table  S4-S5)  [7].  The results were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI to account for their  potential  confounding  effects  on  protein-disease associations. We first extracted all cis-pQTLs that were strongly correlated with the corresponding exposure ( P &lt; 5 × 10 - 8 ). Cis-pQTLs, defined as variants located within ± 1 Mb of the encoding gene, are more likely to directly regulate  protein  expression  and  thereby  reduce  the  risk of  horizontal  pleiotropy  than  trans-pQTLs  [13].  Second, to avoid confounding effects from genomic regions with  complex  linkage  and  immune-related  pleiotropy, we  excluded  all  loci  within  the  extended  major  histocompatibility  complex  (MHC) region (chr6:  26-34  Mb) [8].  We  further  applied  stringent  linkage  disequilibrium

(LD)  clumping  (r 2 &lt;  0.001  within  a  10  Mb  window)  to ensure the independence of the instruments and reduce the  inclusion  of  correlated  variants  potentially  acting through  shared  pathways.  Third,  we  required  genomewide significance ( P &lt; 5 × 10 - 8 ) to ensure robust instrument strength and further evaluated instrument validity using  R 2 and  F  statistics.  Specifically,  R 2 was  calculated as R 2 =  2  ×  EAF × (1 - EAF) × beta 2 ,  where EAF is the effect allele frequency and beta represents the effect size, and the F statistic was then derived as F = R 2 × ( N - 2)/(1 - R 2 ). Variants with F &lt; 10 were excluded to avoid weak or  spurious  instruments  that  might  reflect  confounding or pleiotropic effects. Here, R 2 represents the proportion of  variance  in  protein  levels  explained  by  each  genetic instrument.  For  proteins  with  multiple  instruments,  we selected  those  with  the  highest  total  R 2 to  ensure  that the strongest instruments were used for causal inference [14].  The  cis-pQTLs  from  the  Atherosclerosis  Risk  in Communities (ARIC) and INTERVAL cohorts served as the validation cohorts representing exposed protein drug targets [15, 16]. The ARIC cohort included 2,004 plasma proteins across 7,213 European Americans, whereas the prospective  INTERVAL  cohort  study  included  approximately  50,000  blood  donors  aged  ≥  18  years.  Plasma levels  were  measured  via  a  multiplexed,  aptamer-based approach (SOMAscan assay). The cis-pQTL-related genes from these two cohorts were considered for inclusion in the validation analysis.

## GWAS summary statistics of outcomes

Pooled genetic data related to AD were obtained from the AD progression score [17] and 11 GWASs, with a total of 398,058  individuals  of  European  ancestry  (n case =39,918, ncontrol =358,140)  [18].  For  external  validation,  genetically  aggregated  summary  data  were  obtained  from  the International  Genomics  of  Alzheimer's  Project  (IGAP) (n case =21,982,  n control =41,944)  and  Finngen  (n case =9,301, ncontrol =367,976)  [19,  20].  To  enhance  the  generalizability  and  external  validity  of  our  findings,  East  Asian  AD GWAS (ncase=3,962; ncontrol=4,074) data from the NCGG biobank  were  used  to  explore  drug  targets  among  East Asian individuals [21]. This approach allowed us to investigate  potential  drug  targets  specific  to  East  Asian  individuals while acknowledging the genetic diversity across populations  and  exploring  how  ancestry  may  influence the  relationship  between  genetic  variation  and  drug effects,  particularly  within  the  framework  of  pharmacogenomics.  Additionally,  we  utilized  exome-wide  AD age-at-onset data to perform an exploratory MR analysis [22]. Furthermore, we collected genetic summary data on 7 cognitive phenotypes (including cognitive performance, memory performance, common executive function, reaction time, verbal numerical reasoning, fluid intelligence, and  intelligence,  etc.)  [23-26],  6  biomarkers  (including

CSF Aβ42, CSF p-tau, Aβ-PET, etc.) [17, 27], 26 neuroimaging features (including imaging features such as T1, DTI,  and  rsfMRI  sequences)  [17,  28-32],  and  10  risk factors  (including  smoking,  alcohol  consumption,  education  level,  low-density  lipoprotein  (LDL)  cholesterol and diabetes, etc.) [23, 33-37] to allow for an integrated mechanistic inquiry into the progression of AD in terms of  cognition,  pathology,  brain  structure,  and  function. The reasonableness and availability of data related to AD were considered. Other relevant outcomes were selected on the basis of AD-related characteristics and pathologic features. The detailed information can be found in Table S2.

## Proteome-wide Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

In this study, we used plasma, CSF and brain tissue proteins  as  exposures  and  AD  and  related  outcomes  as endpoints.  If  only  one  pQTL  was  available  for  a  given protein,  the  Wald  ratio  was  used.  We  implemented  an identical  variant  strategy  using  the  same  SNPs  used  in the  primary  analyses  as  a  genetic  tool  and  a  significant variant  strategy  using  genome-wide  significant  SNPs  as a genetic tool to validate the primary findings. To avoid reverse causality, we performed reverse MR analysis and Steiger filtering [38]. Significant p values for the results of reverse MR analysis or Steiger filtering were considered to indicate reverse confounding. Unadjusted, raw P values are also depicted in the text. A threshold of 0.05 was used to indicate a significant association for the false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value.

## Bayesian colocalization analysis and summary-data-based MR (SMR) analysis

To  assess  whether  two  relevant  signals  (protein  and risk  of  AD  and  related  outcomes)  were  consistent  with a  common  causal  variable  to  distinguish  confounding factors  by  linkage  disequilibrium,  we  performed  Bayesian  colocalization  analyses  with  summary  statistics  of protein  and  outcome  GWASs  [39].  The  colocalization analysis included five hypotheses, the fifth of which was that  intraregional  SNPs  share  the  same  causal  variation in  exposure  and  outcome  (PPH 4 ).  For  each  protein,  we included SNPs within ± 1 Mb of the pQTL. When a protein  had  multiple  pQTLs,  colocalization  analyses  were performed separately for each pQTL, and the pQTL with the  strongest  evidence  of  colocalization  was  displayed. The  protein  genetic  locus  was  considered  to  have  the same causal variant when PPH4 &gt;0. 75. While the typical threshold for colocalization is 0.8, we have applied a slightly more lenient threshold to account for the specific context of our study and the available data.

Summary data-based MR (SMR) analysis was used as a  complementary method to further validate the causal relationship between proteins and AD and its associated

outcomes. The heterogeneity-dependent instrument (HEIDI) test, which uses multiple SNPs in a region, was used to distinguish proteins that are associated with outcomes due to shared genetic variation rather than genetic linkage. A p value &lt; 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant effect for the SMR analysis. A p value &gt; 0.05 for the HEIDI test indicated that the association of proteins with the outcome was not driven by linkage disequilibrium.  Combining  Bayesian  colocalization  and  HEIDI tests can help to determine whether the causal effect of a protein target on an outcome is driven by a single locus.

## Mediation analysis

Considering  that  the  identified  drug  targets  may  affect the  risk  of  AD  by  altering  different  biomarkers,  we attempted to model the mediators to explore the possible underlying mechanisms. For proteins that were causally associated  with  both  AD  and  associated  traits,  we  performed mediation analyses to quantify the effect of the protein  on  AD  outcomes  [40].  The  'total'  effect  of  an exposure on an outcome included both the 'direct' effect and any 'indirect' effect through one or more mediators. In  this  study,  total  effects  were  captured  via  standard univariate MR analysis (main MR). To distinguish direct and indirect effects, we used the results of the two-step MR. The indirect effect (mediation) was estimated using the product method, where the effect of the exposure on the mediator was multiplied by the effect of the mediator on  the  outcome  (AD-related  traits).  The  standard  error (SE) of the indirect effect was calculated using the delta method [41]. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was then derived on the basis of the SE, and the p value was calculated  for  the  mediation  effect.  The  mediated  proportion was defined as the ratio of the mediated effect to the total effect. Considering the key role of biomarkers in the AD process, we constructed three mediator models using biomarkers (Aβ and p-tau in CSF or Aβ measurements via PiB-PET) as mediators. The dependent variables were AD, neuroimaging findings, and cognitive function.

## Multiomics analysis and single-cell type expression analysis

Given  that  DNA  is  transcribed  into  mRNA  and  that mRNA is translated into protein, we used MR and colocalization  analyses  to  assess  the  concordance  between protein  and  transcript  level  associations  with  AD.  We obtained cis-expression QTL (eQTL) summary data from 31,684  blood  donors  and  2,856  brain  tissue  individuals from the eQTLGen and BrainMeta v2 databases, respectively.  We  selected  the  identified  drug  target  genes  for MR analysis [42, 43]. Additionally, we obtained brain and blood cis-methylation QTL (mQTL) summary data from the  MetaBrain  Consortium  ( n =  1,160)  and  the  study by Hannon et al. ( n =  1,175) as a supplement to further evaluate  whether  the  biological  mechanisms  of  protein  targets  can  be  influenced  by  methylated  genes  [44, 45].  To  investigate  AD  mechanisms,  we  performed  GO enrichment analysis on the nearest genes to all significant pQTLs, with a specific focus on biological processes (BP), cellular components, and molecular functions. This analysis was conducted via the Metascape (  h  t  t  p  :  /  /  M  e  t  a  s  c  a  p  e  . o  r  g  /  g  p  /  i  n  d  e  x  .  h  t  m  l) web portal, with the background gene list adjusted accordingly.

We  further  assessed the cell type-specific expression of target genes with potential causal effects on AD at  the  protein  locus  level  with  Gene  Expression  Omnibus (GEO) single-nucleus RNA-seq data from the brain tissues of patients with Alzheimer's disease by Dai D et al.  (GSE243292)  [46].  To  minimize  potential  confounding  from  case-control  differences  in  cell  composition, this analysis was restricted to AD samples only, thereby focusing  on  expression  variation  across  different  cell types  within  the  AD  context.  Cells  with  fewer  than  3 counts in a single cell and cells with fewer than 50 counts of  unique  features  were  removed.  The  RNA  TPM  was then  normalized  and  scaled  using  the  'normalize\_total' and 'scale' functions. To test whether the identified genes encoding AD-causing proteins were highly expressed in specific cell types of AD brain tissues, we used differential expression analysis with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to  compare the gene expression levels between one cell type and other cell types. Genes with an average log2-fold change (log2FC) greater than 0.25 and a FDR-adjusted P value less than 0.05 were identified as enriched genes in a particular cell type.

## Protein -protein interaction (PPI), druggability evaluation and Phe-MR analysis

We explored PPI networks in plasma, CSF or brain tissue  that  are  associated  with  AD  risk  (MR  analysis  P FDR &lt; 0.05). Specifically, we aimed to investigate the interactions  between  preferred  proteins  and  whether  proteins identified  via  plasma  data  could  interact  with  proteins identified  via  CSF  or  brain  tissue  data.  Additionally, to  explore  the  interactions  between  these  AD-associated  genes  and  commercially  available  drug  targets,  we selected  3  categories  of  AD  disease-modifying  drugs from  a  recent  review  and  from  the  DrugBank  database (https://www.drugbank.ca)  [47].  Furthermore,  we    e  x  a  m i  n  e  d  relevant  drugs  with  identified  protein  targets  from the  DrugBank  database  to  explore  the  potential  impact of  clinical  drugs  on  AD  and  to  investigate  related  characteristics and the potential for repurposing drugs. Corresponding  drug  targets  were  obtained,  and  currently approved  drugs  that  work  against  identified  potential disease-causing proteins were also searched. Notably, PPI networks  illustrate  functional  associations  and  do  not imply that drugs targeting one protein directly modulate

interacting partners. Moreover, we incorporated 679 disease  phenotypes  with  reference  to  the  study  by  Chong et  al.  [48].  As  in  the  previous  study,  disease  outcomes were  based  on  PheCode  definitions,  a  system  designed to  organize  the  International  Classification  of  Diseases and related health problem codes into phenotypic results suitable for systematic genetic analyses of numerous disease traits. Because of the lack of data availability and sufficient statistical efficacy, we excluded sex-specific related outcomes and outcomes from fewer than 500 patients. In this case, significant Phe-MR results after FDR correction were  normalized  to  changes  in  biomarker  levels  corresponding to a 10% reduction in the risk of AD to facilitate direct comparisons of side effects (magnitude and directionality)  between  biomarkers.  Crucially,  this  method inherently  preserves  therapeutic  directionality:  the  sign of the normalized effect indicates whether the side effect aligns  with  or  opposes  the  intended  therapeutic  action for  AD risk modification. This established methodology is consistent with prior research [48].

## Statistical analysis

When two or more genetic tools were available, inverse variance-weighted MR (MR-IVW) was applied, followed by heterogeneity analysis. The odds ratio (OR) for the risk of  AD  was  expressed  per  one-standard  deviation  (SD) increase in plasma protein levels and per 10-fold increase in CSF and brain tissue protein levels. In the preliminary analysis, multiple tests were adjusted for further analysis using FDR-corrected P values with the Benjamini-Hochberg method. External validation was performed only for initially  identified  proteins,  with  the  p  value  threshold set at 0.05. MR was performed via the 'TwoSampleMR' function (  h t  t p  s  : /  / g  i t  h u  b  . c  o  m  / M  R  C  I  E  U  / T  w  o  S a  m  p  l  e  M R).  Bayesian colocalization analyses were performed via the  'coloc'  package  with  summary  statistics  of  protein and  outcome  GWASs  [39].  SMR  and  HEIDI  tests  were performed via SMR software (SMR v1.0.3) [49]. Preprocessing  and  transformation  of  the  raw  single-nucleus RNA-seq data were performed via the 'scanpy' package in  Python  (version  3.8.3).  The  PPI  analyses  were  performed via the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database version 11.5   (  h  t  t  p  s  :  /  /  s  t  r  i  n g  -  d  b  .  o  r  g  /  )  , with a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 [50].

## Multiomics analysis and cell type-specific expression in brain tissue

At the level of expression of accessible druggable genes, we further validated the directionality of plasma ACADV, ACE,  and  TIMD3  (Table  S34).  The  effects  of  Siglec-3, Siglec-7,  and  Siglec-9  in  CSF  tissue  on  the  risk  of  AD were  also  further  clarified.  At  the  level  of  gene  methylation,  we  detected  significant  genetic  loci  in TIMD3 , ACE , ACADV , Siglec-3 , Siglec-7 , Siglec-9 ,  and APOE2 (Table  S35).  GO  enrichment  analyses  revealed  significant  convergence  of  prioritized  AD  therapeutic  targets in  neuroimmune  pathways  (Table  S36).  These  targets were strongly enriched for macrophage and myeloid cell activation  processes  in  plasma  (P FDR &lt;  0.05),  involving GRN , TREM2 , and HAVCR2 , whereas CSF proteins were significantly  enriched for sialic acid binding and related molecular functions (P FDR &lt;  0.05),  driven by Siglec family  genes  ( CD33 , Siglec-7 ,  and Siglec-9 ).  These  findings highlight  dysregulated  immune  activation  and  glycoimmune checkpoints as potential shared mechanisms warranting further validation. To explore whether the genes encoding the 15 circulating proteins were enriched in the brain tissue of AD patients, we further performed singlecell type expression analysis via single-cell RNA-seq data from the  GEO.  The  cells  were  clustered  into  15  groups and further categorized into 7 cell types (excitatory neurons,  inhibitory  neurons,  astrocytes,  oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte  progenitors,  microglia,  and  endothelial cells) (Fig. 3A). Figure 3B and C show the single-cell expression of these 13 coding genes in each cluster. These protein-coding genes presented cell type-specific enrichment in the brain tissues  of  AD  patients,  with  an  average  Log2FC &gt; 0.25  and  an  FDR &lt; 0.05  (Fig.  3D). PILRA , TIMD3 , Siglec-3 , TREM2 , IDUA , Siglec-7 ,  and Siglec-9 were  enriched  predominantly  in  microglia. GRN was enriched mainly in endothelial cells. APOE was enriched mainly  in  astrocytes  and  microglia. ACE was  enriched mainly in excitatory neurons. BIN1 was enriched in oligodendrocytes. OMGP was enriched in oligodendrocyte progenitors. Additionally, our analysis was based on RNA expression  rather  than  directly  on  protein  expression. While RNA expression can provide valuable insights, it may not fully reflect the actual protein levels or activity.

## PPI network analysis, Phe-MR analysis and druggability evaluation

Our  PPI  network  revealed  direct  interactions  between seven  prioritized  proteins  and  four  targets  of  current therapeutic drugs for AD (Fig. 3E, Table S37-S38). Specifically, we found that BIN1 and TREM2 are associated with  APP  via  the  use  of  STRING.  ACE-ACHE,  GRNAPP, and TIMD3-IL1B may be coexpressed according to text  mining.  Siglec-3  interacts  with  IL1B  and  APP;  and APOE2  is  widely  associated  with  APP,  ACHE,  BCHE, IL1B, NOX4, and NOS1. Additionally, TIMD3 was found to have extensive associations with Siglec isoforms, suggesting that these two types of proteins may be in close proximity but not necessarily in direct contact.

Our normalized Phe-MR results suggest that the identified  drug  targets  have  a  complex  pattern  of  effects  on specific diseases (Table S39-S42). ACADV may increase the  risk  of  developing  nonepithelial  cancer  of  the  skin. PILRA may increase the risk of retinal detachment and symptoms concerning nutrition, metabolism, and development. We found no markedly adverse effects of other drug  targets.  We  also  searched  the  DrugBank  database for  drugs  currently  targeting  the  identified  potential causal proteins. Seven potentially disease-modifying drugs  were  identified:  perindopril,  rescinnamine  and captopril  (3  ACE  inhibitors),  gemtuzumab  ozogamicin and  AVE9633  (2  Siglec-3  regulators),  N-acetyl-alphaD-glucosamine  (1  Siglec-7  regulator),  and  chondroitin sulfate  (1  IDUA  substrate).  Further  longitudinal  studies examining the effects of these drugs will help to clarify the pathogenesis and guide the treatment of AD.

## Discussion

Identifying possible mechanisms for drug targets in AD is a major goal of genetic research. We performed a largescale and multiomics MR analysis of AD and its associated  characteristics.  We  identified  15  candidate  drug targets and categorized them into five tiers on the basis of the strength of genetic evidence, mechanistic evidence and validation across multiple datasets. To demonstrate this tiering strategy, we highlight established AD risk loci such as TREM2 (plasma) and APOE2 (brain), categorized as  Tier  1  and  2,  respectively,  on  the  basis  of  consistent replication  in  two  external  datasets  and  strong  mechanistic  support,  which  were  captured  by  our  framework. Similarly, Siglec-7/9 (CSF), whose inclusion underscores a  potential  mechanism  related  to  CSF  p-tau,  exhibited a  significant  risk  effect  on  AD  progression  and  strongly colocalized with AD phenotypes, and enrichment analyses further indicated its involvement in sialylated glycan and  organic  acid  binding  pathways. Through comprehensive MR analysis of neuroimaging data, cognitive phenotypes, and biomarkers, we identified possible pathways associated with potential drug targets. The possible physiological mechanisms underlying the effects of IDUA (brain), APOE2 (brain), Siglec-7 (CSF) and Siglec-9 (CSF) on AD risk were further explored via mediation analysis. The  methylation  and  transcription  of  these  genes  may be  possible  mechanisms  for  exploring  the  regulation  of AD-related drug targets in  the  future.  Additionally,  our study revealed that the expression of these target genes was mostly enriched in microglia and astrocytes. These genetically predicted druggable targets may be associated with  potential  risks  of  nonepithelial  cancer  of  the  skin, retinal detachment, and metabolism-related diseases.

APOE isoforms are associated with several AD-related phenotypes, such as lipid risk factors and APP processing, which can be used to draw inferences on how it can be  approached  as  a  potential  druggable  target  [11,  51]. Our  integrated  proteomic  MR  analysis  revealed  that APOE2  expression  in  brain  tissue  exerts  widespread beneficial  effects  across  multiple  phenotypes,  including  hippocampal  volume,  cingulum-hippocampus  MD values,  global  structural  connectivity,  and  default  mode network  integrity.  The  APOE2  locus  colocalized  with AD risk signals-a finding that is consistently supported by multiple genomic databases-confirming its high priority  as  a  therapeutic  target.  Mediation  analysis  further indicated that APOE2 may ameliorate AD risk and preserve the integrity of the hippocampal cingulate bundle partly by elevating CSF Aβ42 levels. From a mechanistic perspective,  APOE2  may  confer  protection  against  AD through  multifaceted  biological  pathways  beyond  amyloid clearance [52]. In terms of lipid metabolism, APOE2 enhances  lipid  transport  and  cholesterol  homeostasis, supporting  synaptic  integrity  and  neuronal  membrane stability [53]. Its favorable lipid-binding properties facilitate efficient clearance of cytotoxic lipids and reduce lipid peroxidation, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and oxidative stress [53]. With respect to immunomodulation,  APOE2  appears  to  suppress  proinflammatory pathways-particularly through interactions with IL1Bmodulating  microglial  activation  and  shifting  microglia toward a phagocytic, anti-inflammatory phenotype [54]. This  effect  may  enhance  Aβ  clearance  and  reduce  tau hyperphosphorylation. PPI analyses revealed that APOE2 engages  with  key  players  in  AD  pathogenesis,  including APP, ACHE, BCHE, IL1B, NOX4, and NOS1. These interactions suggest the involvement of amyloid precursor  protein  processing,  cholinergic  signaling,  oxidative stress regulation, and neuroinflammatory cascades. Specifically,  connections  with  NOX4 and NOS1 play a role in  mitigating  reactive  oxygen  species  production  and endothelial  dysfunction,  further  supporting  cerebrovascular health [55, 56]. Taken together, these findings position  APOE2  as  a  multilevel  protective  mediator  in  AD, influencing amyloid pathology, lipid homeostasis, neuroimmune regulation, and redox balance.

The  Siglec  receptors  are  emerging  as  potential  drug targets  in  AD,  as  they  influence  neuroinflammation, immune  surveillance,  and  metabolic  reprogramming. Previous  proteomic  studies  have  shown  that,  as  a  subtype  of  the  Siglec  receptor,  Siglec-3  has  the  potential to  be  an  AD-related  drug  target  protein  [57,  58].  From an  immunological  perspective,  Siglec  receptors  act  as checkpoint  modulators  that  fine-tune  neuroinflammatory responses. Siglec-3 promotes pro-inflammatory microglial  activation,  potentially  accelerating  Aβ  accumulation and impairing phagocytosis [59]. Its interaction with IL1B, as revealed by PPI analysis, suggests a role in amplifying  neuroinflammation  through  cytokine  signaling. Meanwhile, Siglec-7 and Siglec-9-expressed on NK cells  and  microglia-may suppress immune surveillance via  sialic  acid-mediated  inhibitory  signaling  [60].  This dampens  NK  cell  cytotoxicity  and  microglial  clearance of  pathological  proteins  and  senescent  cells,  facilitating p-tau  propagation  and  synaptic  damage.  Metabolically, Siglec  receptors  engage  in  sialic  acid-dependent  metabolic  reprogramming  of  immune  cells  [61].  By  binding to sialylated glycans on neuronal or glial surfaces, Siglecs may alter energy metabolism and mitochondrial function in  microglia,  shifting  them  toward  a  dysfunctional  phenotype that favors tau hyperphosphorylation and oxidative stress [61, 62]. Although currently classified as Tier 3  targets,  Siglec-7  and  Siglec-9  support  a  multipronged disease-modifying strategy for AD therapy through their involvement in innate immune regulation and tau pathology.  These  findings  extend  the  previous  associations  of Siglec-3 with AD by identifying Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 as potential  immunomodulatory  candidates,  suggesting  a broader role of Siglec signaling in tau propagation.

Our results suggest that IDUA may reduce the risk of AD by modulating CSF levels of Aβ42 and p-tau through its  role  in  glycosaminoglycan  (GAG)  catabolism  and lysosomal function. Unlike well-established AD  risk genes, IDUA represents a novel association. Its involvement  in  GAG  catabolism  and  lysosomal  function  provides a mechanistic link to Aβ and tau accumulation that has  not  been  systematically  explored  in  human  genetic studies.  Defects  in  IDUA  cause  mucopolysaccharidosis, and notably, the accumulation of Aβ and p-tau has been observed in mouse models of this disorder [63], suggesting a plausible mechanistic link between impaired GAG degradation  and  AD-related  proteopathy.  IDUA  may influence AD pathogenesis through several pathways. By facilitating the breakdown of GAGs such as heparan sulfate-which is known to promote Aβ aggregation and tau

phosphorylation-IDUA can reduce the burden of pathological protein accumulation [64]. Additionally, by maintaining  lysosomal  function  and  autophagic  flux,  IDUA may  increase  the  clearance  of  misfolded  proteins  and decrease  neuroinflammation  [65].  Improved  lysosomal efficiency  might  also  mitigate  aberrant  microglial  and astrocyte  activation,  thereby  contributing  to  preserved neuronal  integrity.  Although  IDUA  is  currently  classified as a Tier 4 target because of insufficient replication across databases, its biological relevance to protein clearance pathways and lysosomal function warrants further investigation.

Our study  revealed  that  PILRA,  TIMD3,  OMGP,  and ACADV  are  druggable  protein  targets  identified  from plasma  proteomic  analyses,  which  collectively  highlight diverse  pathogenic  pathways  spanning  viral  response, protein  clearance,  and  lipid  metabolism  for  potential therapeutic  intervention  in  AD.  We  found  that  genetically predicted higher levels of the two soluble isoforms of PILRA (deltaTM and M14) in plasma were associated with  a  lower  risk  of  AD.  Mechanistically,  PILRA  may confer protection by limiting HSV-1 viral entry through reduced  interaction  with  viral  glycoprotein  D,  thereby attenuating  neuroinflammation  and  Aβ  accumulation triggered  by  herpesviral  infection  [66].  TIMD3,  which binds to phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cells and interacts  with  amyloid  precursor  protein,  may  help  regulate the clearance of cellular debris and modulate amyloidogenic processing [18]. OMGP, an oligodendrocyte myelin glycoprotein enriched in the CNS, was suggestively protective against AD and associated with improved reaction time,  highlighting  its  potential  role  in  preserving  white matter integrity and cognitive processing speed [67, 68]. Conversely,  elevated  plasma  ACADV  levels  correlated with worsened cognitive performance, possibly through disruptions  in  fatty  acid  oxidation  and  mitochondrial energy  metabolism,  leading  to  impaired  neuronal  function.  Several  plasma  targets  (PILRA,  ACE,  TREM2, BIN1,  and  GRN)  have  been  previously  reported  in  MR studies, strengthening their credibility [10, 11, 69]. These findings were extended by text mining: ACE may coregulate pathways with APOE and ACHE; BIN1 and TREM2 are  strongly  tied  to  amyloid  plaque  accumulation;  and GRN supports lysosomal function and Notch signaling, facilitating  the  clearance  of  misfolded  proteins [70]. We observed that higher plasma TREM2 levels were associated with a delayed age at onset of AD, potentially reflecting  its  role  in  enhancing  microglial  phagocytosis  and promoting amyloid clearance. Tier assignment highlights robust targets such as PILRA with antiviral mechanisms, whereas tentative targets such as BIN1 are supported by weaker colocalization evidence. Collectively, these results highlight  cell  type-specific  mechanisms-spanning  viral response,  lipid  metabolism,  myelination,  and  protein clearance-through  which  these  candidates  may  influence AD risk.

While recent proteomic studies have expanded the list of  potential  drug  targets  [10,  11,  58],  our  study  stands apart  by  linking  each  target  to  causal  genetic  pathways and  assessing  their  druggability  potential.  For  instance, the  analysis  of  APOE2  demonstrated  how  our  integrative framework can elucidate the underlying mechanisms of  genetic  protection  against  AD.  Our  study  further revealed that APOE2 in brain tissue may confer protection by increasing CSF Aβ42 levels, thereby reducing the genetic  risk  of  AD  and  improving  neuroimaging  metrics. Our findings also suggest that APOE2 is potentially coexpressed with the ACHE target of donepezil and the APP target of lucanemab and aducanemab, highlighting its  promising  druggability  potential  and  offering  specific  mechanistic  insights  for  drug  development.  This approach ensures that drug targets are not only identified but also mechanistically understood, making them more suitable  for  therapeutic  intervention.  Recent  work  by Ali et al. prioritized candidate genes on the basis of CSF proteomic profiling, providing direct evidence of protein alterations  in  the  CNS  [71].  In  contrast,  our  integrative framework  combined  proteogenomic  causal  inference and MR across plasma, CSF, and brain pQTLs, alongside neuroimaging  and  cognitive  data.  Our  framework  successfully recapitulates well-established AD-associated proteins such as APOE and TREM2 while revealing novel candidates, including Siglec-7, Siglec-9, and IDUA. These findings  not  only  extend  previous  proteomic  observations  but  also  provide  complementary  mechanistic insights into AD pathophysiology, highlighting potential targets  for  therapeutic  intervention.  These  complementary perspectives highlight the need to integrate diverse proteomic strategies to comprehensively map AD biology and therapeutic opportunities.

Despite the inherent trade-off between resolution and robustness, proteomic MR capitalizes on existing largescale  GWAS  resources  and  thereby  circumvents  additional  experimental  costs.  More  importantly,  it  enables discovery in settings where sample size cannot be feasibly  expanded, such as rare diseases or hard-to-measure phenotypes, offering insights that traditional large-scale studies  are  unlikely  to  achieve  [8,  9,  72].  By  combining  multitrait  data,  tissue-specific  pQTLs,  and  annotations,  proteomic  MR  improves  the  detection  of  causal signals beyond the reach of GWAS or proteomics alone [73,  74].  Evidence  increasingly  shows  that  therapeutic targets  supported by human genetics are more likely to succeed in clinical development [75]. Proteomic MR has already  contributed  to  this  process  by  accelerating  target  prioritization  and  validation  across  diverse  disease areas, with several findings subsequently corroborated in independent  cohorts  and  clinical  studies,  underscoring

its  translational  value  from  genetic  discovery  to  clinically  actionable  therapies  [76,  77].  We  explored  protein targets  for  genetic  prediction  of  AD  in  terms  of  a  wide range  of  cognition-related  phenotypes,  which  provides researchers with a broad range of associations with ADrelated  mechanisms.  Our  tiered  framework  provides  a systematic way to prioritize candidate genes from GWAS loci while offering insights into AD biology. This layered strategy enables us not only to refine genetic associations but  also  to  generate  hypotheses  for  downstream  functional studies [78].

Despite  being  the  large-scale  and  multiomics  study of  AD,  it  has  several  limitations.  The  lack  of  significant MR proteins  in  East  Asians  may  stem  from  lower  statistical  power  because of sample size differences, rather than  biological  differences,  highlighting  the  need  for further research. In addition, our study cannot precisely pinpoint  individual  pleiotropic  variants  (e.g.,  APOE2), which  may  affect  the  interpretation  of  our  results,  particularly  for  genes  with  known  complex  effects.  Additionally, although MR provides stronger causal evidence than observational designs do, its inferential limits caution  against  claiming  definitive  causation.  Because  the GWASs  used  in  this  study  were  based  on  the  lifetime risk of neurodegenerative disease at the population level, prospective studies tracking protein trajectories prior to diagnosis  are  needed.  Furthermore,  compared  to  previous proteome-wide MR studies, the tissue specificity and isoform  associations  of  drug-target  proteins  may  exert divergent  effects  on  AD  risk,  as  exemplified  by  PILRA and  APOE.  This  underscores  the  imperative  for  future proteomic  investigations  to  prioritize  isoform-specific and tissue-resolved analyses in order to more accurately delineate causal pathways and identify promising therapeutic targets for AD.

## Conclusion

In summary, this multiomics proteomic MR  study revealed 15 high-priority druggable targets for Alzheimer's disease, with causal effects quantified across biomarkers, neuroimaging and cognitive functions. Crucially, we elucidated Aβ42- or p-tau-mediated pathogenic mechanisms for novel targets such as IDUA or Siglec-7/9. These findings offer a translatable blueprint for accelerating targeted therapy discovery, urging functional validation and clinical translation.
